=== rosie_pirates
titre: Rosie, Captain for a Day

Aboard The Seashell, nobody is faster than Rosie. She scampers up the mast in three hops, leaps over barrels, ties ten knots a minute, and is always finished before everyone else.
“If I were the captain,” she boasts, paws on her hips, “we would go twice as fast!”
One morning, Captain Mirabelle overhears her. Calmly, she takes off her big blue hat and places it over Rosie’s long ears. “You’re on! Today, you are in charge. As for me, I’m off to have a nap in my hammock.”

With a bound, Rosie perches on a barrel. “Listen up, crew! Set sail for Pineapple Island! Ready, steady, GO!”
“You, hoist the sail! Faster! You, scrub the deck! No, not like that! And you… give me that broom! Out of the way, I’ll do it myself!”
The crew bustles about, running and bumping into each other, buckets and ropes spilling over their paws. Rosie fires off thirty orders a minute. She chatters so quickly that she cannot hear anything besides her own voice.

“Um… Captain Rosie,” starts Nestor, raising a paw, “the wind is turning, maybe we should…” “No time!” “Captain Rosie,” tries the cook, ladle in hand, “about lunch…” “Later!”
Right at the top of the mast, a tiny voice tries next. It is Sardine, the lookout mouse, leaning out of the crow’s nest. “Captain! I can see something, straight ahead!”
Rosie puts a paw to her lips. “Shh, Sardine! I am in charge, I know best! Faster with that sail!”

The Seashell cuts through the waves like an arrow. Rosie is thrilled. Right ahead, a big white cloud has settled over the sea, thick as cotton wool. Fog. “Straight through it! Full steam ahead!”
“Captain!” squeaks Sardine at the top of her voice. “In the fog, there are…” “I said shh!” Sardine opens her mouth. Closes it. Then she sits back down, ears drooping, and stays silent.
The fog swallows the boat completely. You cannot even see the tip of your snout, barely the faint glow of the lantern. Everything is white, everything is quiet. A little too quiet.

CRUNCH! Everyone tumbles onto the deck, paws flailing in the air. The Seashell is wedged between two rocks, tilted like a plate stacked all wonky.
“Rocks!” gasps Rosie, her hat askew. “Why didn’t anyone warn me?” The crew stares at her without a word. Nobody answers.
Then Sardine raises a timid paw. Her voice is very tiny: “I did try, Captain. Twice.” Rosie feels her big ears turn very, very warm.

Rosie slumps down onto the deck. The big hat slips over her eyes. It is heavy, after all. Much heavier than it looks.
She lifts the brim of the hat, wanting to bark an order. But which one? Her mouth opens, but nothing comes out. For the first time in her life, Rosie is completely speechless.
And in the silence, with one ear pricked up, she hears things. The creaking of the wood. The water lapping against the hull. And her crew whispering: “I’ve got an idea…” “Me too…”

Rosie takes off the hat and holds it between her paws. “I’m listening.” Saying those words feels very strange in her mouth. “Nestor?” “Well… If we all push on the same side, just as the wave comes in…”
“Sardine?” “From up here, I spotted a way through the rocks! I can guide you.” “And I,” adds the cook, waving a big block, “have butter to make the hull slide!”
A wave swells behind them. “All together!” calls Rosie. “One, two, three… PUSH!” They push, shoulder to shoulder. And whoosh-slide! The Seashell slips free, rights itself, and floats.

“To the left!” shouts Sardine. Rosie turns left. “Straight on!” Rosie goes straight on. And suddenly, the fog parts like a curtain: there is the sun, and Pineapple Island, bright green and lovely.
In her hammock, Captain Mirabelle stretches just at that moment, one eye open, the other closed. Well… perhaps she wasn’t truly asleep. “So, Captain Rosie? How does it feel to be in charge?”
Rosie hands back her hat. “It takes a lot of listening,” she answers with a laugh. “Lucky I’ve got big ears!”


=== rosie_dino
titre: Rosie and the Moon Flowers

In Fern Valley, Rosie does everything on the run. She eats on the run, she says hello on the run, and they even say she sleeps on the run! Behind her, all that is left is a little cloud of dust.
This morning, big news: right at the very top of Blue Hill, the moon flowers are going to open. They only bloom once a year, just as the sun goes down.
“I’ll be first! One, two, three...” “Hold your horses!” her mummy stops her. “You are going with Granny Dipla. She knows the way.” Rosie’s ears droop. “Oh no... Not Granny Dipla!”

Granny Dipla is the oldest diplodocus in the valley, as tall as a hill. She is ever so kind. And she is very, very, very slow.
She lifts one foot... puts it down... lifts the other foot... Meanwhile, Rosie has already galloped round a tree twelve times, making herself quite dizzy!
“Faster, Granny Dipla! We’ll miss everything!” “Heh heh... All in good time, my poppet. Oh, look at that beetle, how shiny it is!” Arms crossed, Rosie taps her foot. She does not look.

After an hour, Rosie cannot stand it any longer. Just then, the path splits in two. “I’m taking the shortcut!” she calls, waving her paw. “See you up there!”
“Rosie, wait! That path...” But Rosie is already gone. Zoom! With her ears pinned back, she dashes off so fast that the ferns barely see her go by.
She jumps over a puddle. Hop! Two puddles. Hop! Then a third one, wider, browner, shinier. And that one... she doesn't jump quite far enough.

SPLODGE! There goes Rosie, belly-deep in the mud. It is the Sticky Swamp, and it certainly lives up to its name! She tugs on one paw: stuck. On the other: even stickier!
“Ready, steady... go!” She wriggles, she wiggles, she paddles, splashing the frogs all around. But the more she hurries, the deeper she sinks.
Over yonder, the sun is starting to slip behind the hill. The moon flowers will open without her. And Rosie, the fastest little thing in all the valley, cannot budge an inch.

Boom... Boom... A steady footstep makes the ferns tremble. Then a long, long neck bends over the swamp, with a pair of little shiny spectacles perched right at the end.
“There you are, my poppet.” Granny Dipla does not say “I told you so.” She lowers her big head, very gently, right down to Rosie’s paws. “Hold tight. And no wriggling.”
Rosie holds on tight. Granny Dipla lifts her, slowly, slowly. The mud goes slurp, then plop! And Rosie finds herself perched right up high, dripping wet, on the tallest back in the world.

“Now, we go at my pace,” Granny Dipla announces. Rosie heaves a big sigh. But she is far too tired to run. So, for once, she looks around.
She spots a dragonfly drying its wings in the sunshine. Further away, the river weaves and winds, looping like a ribbon. And when Granny crushes the mint beneath her feet, Rosie closes her eyes to breathe in the sweet scent.
“Was all of this here before?” asks Rosie, lying flat on her tummy on the long neck. “Always, my poppet. But you cannot see it when you’re running.”

The sun is already skimming the hill when they reach the top. Rosie tiptoes as high as she can, her heart thumping. “Are we late? Have we missed it all?”
“Hush,” whispers Granny Dipla. “Sit down here. Now, you’ll have to do the hardest thing in the world.” “Run?” “No, my poppet. Wait.”
Rosie sits down. Her paws are tingling, itching to go. She closes her eyes and counts in her head: one... two... three... And she stays right there.

Then, one by one, without any rush, the moon flowers unfold their petals. They are white, they are blue, glowing in the dusk as if each one had swallowed a tiny star.
Rosie does not twitch a single whisker. Not a word. She watches right until the very last flower, which blooms right in front of her nose. It takes time. It is wonderful.
On the way home, under the stars, she walks alongside Granny Dipla. One foot... then the other... “Not running, my poppet?” “Not tonight,” whispers Rosie. “Tonight, I don't want to get there.”


=== rosie_chevaliers
titre: Rosie and the Squires' Race

At Highrock Castle, the grand squires' race takes place every year. And every year, Rosie wins, paws down!
Three golden cups sit proudly on her shelf. Every evening by candlelight, she polishes them until they sparkle. She has even measured out the space for the fourth one.
“Winning is easy,” she tells anyone who will listen, her nose in the air. “You just have to be me!”

This year, though, there is a newcomer on the starting line. It is Pip, the little hedgehog. He has tiny paws, scruffy prickles, and barely reaches Rosie’s knees.
“Are you going to run?” Rosie giggles behind her paw. “I am going to try,” Pip answers bravely. “I trained all winter long.”
Queen Hermine raises her white handkerchief. Hush... “One... two... three... GO!”

Rosie shoots forward like an arrow. She is soon so far ahead that she takes time to wave to the crowd. She even does a little twirl. “Look at me!”
But on the big downhill slope, a funny noise tickles her ears. Whirrrrrr... Pip has rolled into a ball. And a ball on a slope... rolls!
Whirrrrr-ZOOM! The prickly ball zooms right past her, darts across the little stone bridge, and crosses the finish line first. By a tiny hedgehog nose!

“The winner is Pip!” The crowd bursts into cheers. They hand him the golden cup and carry the little hedgehog in triumph. Well... with thick gloves on!
Rosie stands frozen on the line, stiff as a board. Second place. She has never been second, never! It feels like a pebble in her shoe, but all over.
“That's cheating!” she fumes. “Rolling doesn't count! The grass was slippery! The sun was in my eyes!” She stomps her foot so hard that it hurts, and hops about on one paw.

Pip potters over, clutching his cup tightly. He holds out his tiny paw. “Well done, Rosie. You run ever so fast. I just got lucky on the slope.”
Rosie glares at the outstretched paw. She crosses her arms. “I don't shake paws with cheats,” she huffs. And she turns on her heel, her nose in the air.
Behind her, Pip is left all alone with his cup. His smile goes out, like a blown-out candle.

That evening in her bedroom, Rosie gazes at her three cups. How strange: they seem less shiny than usual. The space for the fourth is empty, and sleep will not come. So she presses her nose against the window.
Down below in the courtyard, a little light flickers. It is Pip, with his lantern. All alone in the dark, he runs, he rolls, bump! He knocks into the well, rubs his head, gets back up, and starts all over again.
“All winter long...” Rosie whispers, holding her polishing cloth in her paws. He trained all winter long. Whilst she was just polishing her cups.

The next morning, Rosie walks across the courtyard, her paws heavy as stones. That little walk is harder than any race. She leans towards the little hedgehog and holds out her paw. “Pip...”
“You didn't cheat yesterday. You won fair and square. And I... was a very sore loser.” Looking sheepish, her ears drooping low, she offers her paw. “Well done. Truly.”
Pip shakes her paw. Ouch! It tickles and pricks just a little bit. “Will you teach me how to roll?” asks Rosie, shaking her fingers. “Only if you teach me how to turn!” giggles Pip. “I bump into everything!”

All through the spring, they can be seen rolling down the hill together. Rosie is dreadful at rolling: her ears stick out and flap like flags. As for Pip, he takes his corners better and better.
The following year, who won the grand race? Some swear it was Rosie. Others bet it was Pip. To tell the truth, they were both laughing so hard as they crossed the tape that nobody really noticed.
On Rosie’s shelf, there are still three cups. And in the space for the fourth, in a neat little frame, is a drawing. A bunny and a hedgehog, rolling together in the grass.


=== rosie_espace
titre: Rosie and the Big Red Temper

Today, Rosie and Bip the robot are blasting off for Marshmallow Planet. The rocket is all ready, and seatbelts are fastened tight. Rosie pushes the big green button. “One, two, three… blast off!”
Nothing. The rocket just goes “putt”, and that is all.
Rosie presses it again. “Putt.” She presses harder, with both paws. “Putt, putt.” Inside her tummy, something begins to warm up, very softly, like a saucepan on the hob.

“Checks in progress,” announces Bip in his calm voice. “Please be patient.” Patient! Rosie hates that word. It gets hotter in her tummy. It climbs up into her chest. It bubbles away.
Her cheeks turn pink. Then red. Then tomato-red. Her ears shoot up, as stiff as two sticks. Her paws ball into tight little fists all by themselves.
It is the big red temper. When it arrives, smoke almost puffs from her ears, and Rosie cannot stop anything at all. It is just like a rocket: once launched…

“WORK, YOU SILLY ROCKET!” Bang! She drums on the dashboard. Bang! On the blue button, the yellow one, the purple one. Bang, bang, bang! On all the buttons at once, flashing in a panic.
“Be careful!” warns Bip, waving his arms wide. “Whatever you do, do not touch the red bu—” BANG.
A tiny click. Then a great big silence. Then Rosie feels her paws lift off the floor, and her ears float up into the air.

She has switched off gravity: nothing stays on the floor any more. Everything floats away, the carrots, the screwdrivers, the socks, and lunchtime soup, drifting about in big orange bubbles.
Rosie spins slowly round, head down, feet in the air. She wants to bang things again, but in mid-air, there is nothing to thump. Her anger kicks at empty space.
And worst of all: Bip’s toolbox has popped open. His spare bolts scatter across the cabin like a swarm of tiny flies. Bip stretches out his little arms. Too short: he cannot catch a single one.

“My bolts…” crackles Bip in a tiny little voice. In the muddle, a carrot has bumped his left arm. It dangles all crooked, like a broken twig.
Rosie looks at him, paws over her mouth. The red temper is still there, but behind it, something else creeps in. Something cold and very small. “Did I do that?” she whispers.
“Affirmative,” replies Bip gently. “But you were not really you. You were the anger. Would you like me to show you how I cool my circuits down?”

“When I get too hot,” explains Bip, “I puff. Three times. Like this.” Pfffft… On his back, a little fan begins to purr.
Rosie does not have a fan. But she has her tummy, round and soft under her paws. She closes her eyes. “One…” Pfffft. “Two…” Pfffft. “Three…” Pfffffffffft.
She feels the saucepan cool right down. Her paws uncurl. Her ears flop back down. The red temper turns pink, then teeny-tiny, and then floats out through the porthole like a little cloud, drifting away among the stars.

“I’m sorry, Bip.” Rosie gathers the bolts one by one, like butterflies, and pops them into a jar. Then she screws the robot’s arm back on, ever so gently, poking out her tongue.
Then she presses the red button again. Clatter! Down tumbles everything at once, the socks, the screwdrivers… and the soup. Never mind: they will eat carrots instead.
“Check complete,” announces Bip, pointing to a red lever. “The rocket would not start because the handbrake was on.” Rosie bursts out laughing, clutching her tummy. All of that just for a handbrake!

She releases the brake. Then, with just one finger, ever so gently, she presses the green button. “One, two, three… blast off!” And WHOOSH! The rocket leaps among the stars, straight towards Marshmallow Planet.
The red temper came back, of course. It always does: when a shoelace gets tangled, when a block tower tumbles down, when you lose a game of Ludo.
But now, when Rosie feels the saucepan heating up, she closes her eyes and rests her paws on her tummy. One… two… three. And Bip, snuggled beside her, lets his little fan purr. Just to keep her company.


=== rosie_foret
titre: Rosie and Her Little Gluepot

Rosie has a little brother. His name? Pompon. He is knee-high to three carrots, has two tiny short ears, and just one big idea in life: to do everything Rosie does.
If Rosie jumps, Pompon jumps. If Rosie scratches her ear, Pompon scratches his ear. If Rosie says “Bother!”, Pompon says “Bother!”, and Rosie is the one who gets told off.
“Mummy! He won’t leave me alone!” groans Rosie. “He isn’t a brother, he’s a little gluepot!” “Gluepot!” repeats Pompon, delighted, safely snuggled against Mummy.

Today, Rosie has a big-girl plan: with Filou and Hazel, she is going to climb to the very top of Strawberry Hill, where the biggest, sweetest wild strawberries grow.
“Me too! Me too!” begs Pompon, standing on the tips of his paws. Rosie shakes her head. “No, Pompon. It’s too far. You’re too little. You walk far too slowly.”
“Take your brother,” Mummy decides. “And hold his paw tightly.” Rosie rolls her eyes and lets out an enormous sigh. Her whole day is ruined.

Along the path, Pompon stops at every single step. For a pebble. For an ant. For a wee. To say hello, nose-to-horns, to a snail that didn’t ask for anything at all.
“Hurry up!” Filou and Hazel are already far ahead. Rosie stamps her feet. Pompon is admiring a pebble.
Then Rosie has an idea. Not a very nice idea. “Pompon, let’s play hide-and-seek!” she suggests in a syrupy-sweet voice. “Count to ten, right here, against this big oak tree. And don’t move an inch.”

Pompon is overjoyed. Rosie is playing with him! Quickly, nose pressed against the bark, he covers his eyes. “One… two… three… five…”
Rosie slips away on tiptoe. Soon, she catches up with her friends. She runs, she climbs, she laughs. Free at last! “I’ll come back and find him in a bit,” she promises herself.
The strawberries are huge, red, and sweet. Rosie gobbles down one, two, ten of them, with juice dripping all over her chin. But funny thing: by the eleventh, they don’t taste like much at all.

The sky turns orange. “Your brother’s taking an awfully long time to count,” remarks Hazel. Rosie looks down. The forest is vast, and already so dark. And Pompon is very small.
She remembers his little face earlier on. So happy to be playing. The strawberry drops from her paws. In her tummy, all the others turn into heavy stones.
Rosie rushes down the hill. She has never run so fast in her life. There is the big oak tree. She circles it. Once. Twice. Nobody. Nothing but flattened grass.

“Pompon?” Her voice sounds tiny in the big forest. She cups her paws around her mouth. “POMPON!” Nothing. Just an owl waking up.
In the soft earth, there are pawprints, teeny-tiny ones. Rosie follows them, her nose close to the ground. They wander, they wobble, they head towards the stream. And there, caught on a bramble, is a scrap of blue cloth. His bib.
Rosie clutches it to her heart. “He isn’t a gluepot,” she says out loud. “He’s my brother.” A tear rolls down her cheek. She is very, very scared.

A little noise. Sniff. Under a tall fern, a small white bundle is shivering. “Ten! I counted to ten,” sniffs Pompon. “Lots and lots of times. But you didn’t come. So I went looking for you.”
Rosie hugs him so tightly that he squeaks, “Squeak!” “I’m sorry, Pompon. That wasn’t real hide-and-seek. I left you all alone. It was mean. I’ll never, ever do it again.”
Pompon opens his paw. In the middle is a wild strawberry, tiny and completely squished. “I found it. It’s for you,” he announces proudly. It is the best strawberry Rosie has ever tasted.

She carries him home on her back, through the fireflies. Long before they reach the house, he falls asleep, his nose tucked into her neck. He is quite heavy, after all, for someone only three carrots tall.
The following Sunday, Rosie goes back to Strawberry Hill. Slowly. Very slowly. Pausing for every pebble, every ant, and every snail.
“Who’s the little fellow following you everywhere?” asks a passing hedgehog. Rosie takes Pompon’s paw in hers. “Him? He’s my little gluepot. And nobody else is allowed to call him that.”


=== rosie_ocean
titre: Rosie and the Promise to Pearl

Right at the bottom of the ocean, on Thousand Bubbles Reef, Rosie has a tiny friend: Pearl the seahorse. Pearl is so shy that she turns pink right to the tip of her tail whenever anyone looks at her.
Yet Pearl has a secret: she dances. She twirls, she glides, she makes loops of bubbles. And on Saturday, for the very first time, she will dance on the seashell stage, in front of the whole reef.
“I’ve got stage fright, Rosie, I’m so scared... Will you come? If you’re in the front row, I know I can do it.” Rosie puts a paw over her heart. “Cross my heart, I promise! I’ll be there before everyone else!”

Saturday arrives. Rosie is swimming towards the reef when three cheeky dolphins block her way. “Hey, speedy! Great Current Race! The winner gets this coral crown!”
A race! Rosie’s paws wriggle all on their own. “Well, the thing is... I promised a friend...” she stammers. “It only takes five minutes!” tease the dolphins. “Are you scared of losing?”
Scared of losing, her? Never in a million years! “Five minutes,” thinks Rosie. “I’ve got plenty of time. One, two, three...”

Zoom! The currents sweep her away in a swirl of bubbles. It’s like a water slide that never ends. She overtakes one dolphin, then two, then three. “One more lap!” they call out.
One more lap. Then a rematch. Then a rematch of the rematch. Rosie wins the coral crown! She rests it between her two ears and puffs out her chest, as proud as a queen.
Just then, she spots the light up on the surface. It isn’t yellow anymore: it’s orange. Evening is falling. Rosie slaps her paws to her cheeks. “The show!”

Meanwhile, back on the reef, everyone has settled into their shell. The crowd falls quiet. The seaweed curtain opens. Pearl steps onto the stage, looking tiny.
She looks at the front row. One shell has stayed empty. She looks at the second row. At the very back. Everywhere. Rosie isn’t there.
The music begins. Pearl tries to twirl. But her fins are trembling far, far too much. She turns pale, as pale as sand. And she darts off to hide behind a big yellow sponge, without having danced at all.

Rosie swims as fast as her paws can carry her, completely out of breath. Too late: when she arrives, every shell is empty. Only Octave the octopus is still there, sweeping the stage with three of his arms.
“The show?” sighs Octave. “Oh, it didn’t happen. The little one was waiting for someone.” His round eye settles on the coral crown. “Pretty crown.”
Suddenly, the crown feels as heavy as a boulder on Rosie’s head.

She finally tracks down Pearl, curled up right inside a pink sea anemone. “Pearl! Look, I won a crown, you can have it!” Pearl doesn’t turn around.
“I don’t want a crown. I wanted you to be there. You said cross your heart, promise.” Her little voice doesn’t shout; it barely trembles. That makes it worse.
Rosie wants to explain. The dolphins, the five minutes, the rematch. But sitting in the sand, with the crown dropped beside her, she knows deep down that all her excuses are much smaller than her promise.

“You’re right,” whispers Rosie, her ears drooping. “A promise isn’t ‘if I have time’. I was wrong, Pearl. I can’t redo Saturday. But can I try to make it right?”
All night long, through the dark water twinkling with little lights, Rosie dashes from cave to cave. She knocks on the crabs’ door, the sunfish’s door, the old turtle’s door. “Tomorrow evening! Please, everyone come back! Please!”
The next day, Octave hangs the curtain back up with all eight arms. And Rosie arrives two hours early. She sits in the front row, paws crossed, and doesn’t budge. Not even when three dolphins swim past, whistling.

The curtain opens. Pearl steps forward, tiny as ever. She looks at the front row... and this time, she spots two big ears, and two waving paws.
So Pearl dances. She twirls, she glides, she weaves loops of bubbles that rise and rise right to the top of the sea. Open-mouthed, the whole reef holds its breath. Rosie claps until her paws tingle.
And the coral crown? Rosie hung it above the stage, for Pearl. And from that day on, whenever she says “I promise”, she never needs to say “cross my heart”. Her word is always enough.


=== rosie_noel
titre: Rosie and the Far-Too-Long Scarf

Christmas is in three days. Rosie wants to give her mummy the loveliest present in the whole forest. Her nose pressed against Margot the magpie's shop window, she has already chosen it: a golden teapot that shines just like a little sun.
Smash! She breaks her piggy bank. Out tumble two buttons, a pebble, and a single shiny coin. But the teapot costs a hundred coins!
“You could just make her present,” suggests Pompon, pointing to the knitting basket. Rosie shrugs. Make one! A homemade present doesn't sparkle!

Still, she has no choice. She finds a ball of red wool, two big knitting needles, and decides to knit a scarf. “Ready, steady, go!”
Rosie knits the way she runs: at top speed. Click, click, click, click! One stitch knit, one stitch wonky, one stitch who-knows-what.
She knits in the morning, she knits at lunchtime, and again at night, tucked under her blanket with a torch. She doesn't count the rows. She has completely forgotten to stop!

On Christmas Eve, the scarf is finished. Rosie unrolls it. She unrolls it some more. And more. The scarf winds all the way round the bedroom. Twice!
Far too long. Much too wide at one end, skinny as a twig at the other. And right in the middle yawns a big hole, right where Pompon sneezed.
“It's ugly,” whispers Rosie. And her ears droop, all floppy and sad.

Outside, she passes her neighbours. The hedgehogs carry a parcel tied with a silver ribbon. The squirrels have a varnished wooden music box. Everything is shiny, everything is perfect.
Rosie hides her scarf behind her back. Her cheeks burn hot. “Mummy deserved a golden teapot,” she mumbles. “And she's getting… this.”
That evening, she stuffs the scarf right at the bottom of the cupboard, under a heap of old socks. “I'll just say I ran out of time,” she decides.

On Christmas morning, everyone hugs. Mummy unfolds Pompon's drawing: a big green scribble. “Oh! A magnificent… Christmas tree?” “It's you, Mummy!” “Ah. Yes, it looks just like me.”
“And what about you, my Rosie?” Rosie stares down at her paws. “I… haven't got anything,” she stammers. “I ran out of time.”
“Yes she has!” pipes up Pompon. “She made a great big red snake! It's in the cupboard!” And before Rosie can stop him, back he comes, dragging the scarf behind him in a shower of socks.

Rosie wishes she could vanish under the rug. “Don't look,” she pleads. “It's ruined. Too long, and it's got a hole. I wanted to give you the golden teapot, but…”
Mummy stays quiet. Slowly, she runs her paws over the wool. Over every wonky stitch. “Did you make this all by yourself? All those nights when I saw light under your blanket?”
Rosie nods, feeling very sheepish. And then she notices something wonderful: Mummy's eyes are shining. Much brighter than the teapot.

Mummy winds the scarf round her neck. Once. Twice. There is still plenty left, trailing along the floor like a giant ribbon. So she winds it round Rosie. And then round Pompon.
“Look. A teapot is only for one person. Your scarf keeps all three of us warm. And the hole…” Pompon pokes his head through, giggling. “It's my window!”
Off they go for a walk in the snow, all three bundled up in the same red scarf. From afar, they look just like a funny six-legged creature.

They stroll past Margot's shop window. The golden teapot is still there. Rosie turns her head. Yes, it sparkles. But it looks rather cold, all by itself behind the glass.
All winter long, Mummy wears the scarf. To go to the market, to hang out the washing, and even once to bed. If anyone tells her it is too long, she proudly replies: “It's just the right size.”
And what about Rosie? She has picked up her needles again. Click… click… click. Slower this time, poking her tongue out. She is knitting mittens for Pompon. So far, there is one. With six fingers.


=== rosie_halloween
titre: Rosie and the Melted Costume

This year, Rosie is going to win the Halloween fancy-dress competition. It is decided! For a whole month, she has been working in secret, by lamplight, in the garden shed.
It is a dragon. A magnificent dragon, made entirely of paper: green scales glued on one by one, a spiky tail, and wings that fan out wide.
“It is perfect,” Rosie cheers, her paws on her hips. “Not a single scale out of place. Nobody has ever seen a costume like this!”

On Halloween night, she slips into her dragon suit. “Ready, steady, go!” Off she goes to the clearing. As she passes, everybody turns to look. “Oooh!” Rosie struts along, her head held high.
She does not see the big black cloud gathering behind her. Plip! A drop on her nose. Plip, plop! Two drops on her wings.
And suddenly, all the rain in the sky comes pouring down on the forest.

Rosie dashes away. But paper does not like water! The scales peel off, one by one. The green paint trickles down her legs. The tail dangles like an old soggy sock.
When she finally shelters under the great oak tree, she is no longer a dragon. She is a completely green bunny, soaked to the bone, with bits of soggy paper stuck all over her.
“My dragon! A whole month’s work! It’s completely ruined! This is the worst night of my life!” She flops down into a puddle, her ears flat. Rosie doesn't want anything anymore.

Her friends come running up. “Take my pumpkin hat,” offers Nestor. “And my pirate bandana!” adds Filou. Plume, without a word, holds out her witch’s cloak.
“No! That’s not what I wanted! I wanted MY dragon, just like I planned!” Rosie crosses her arms and turns her back on them. “I’m going home. Halloween is cancelled.”
From the corner of her eye, she looks at her green paws. The soggy bits of paper. The cloak, the bandana, the hat, still held out by her friends. Inside her head, a tiny little idea begins to sprout.

She peers into the puddle. Looking back at her is a dripping, green creature covered in tatters. It isn’t a dragon. It is… something else.
“Well now,” whispers Rosie. “I look just like a swamp monster.” In one swift move, she throws the cloak over her shoulders. Then the bandana. And the pumpkin hat on top! “A swamp monster who is a witch and a pirate.”
Filou giggles. “With some seaweed, it would be even better!” Rosie scoops up a handful of wet leaves and slaps them onto her ears. Splat!

Everyone joins in! Nestor draws muddy whiskers on her cheeks. Plume gives her an acorn necklace. Rosie sticks a twig onto her head for a horn.
The more they add, the more dreadful it looks. The more dreadful it looks, the more they roar with laughter! Rosie hadn't planned any of this. And that is exactly what makes it so funny.
“What is your monster called?” asks Plume. Rosie thinks for a moment, then lifts her paw solemnly. “The Swamp Gloobidragon. It only exists one night a year. And only when it rains!”

The rain stops. The competition begins. Across the stage parade very tidy ghosts, neatly pressed witches, and an immaculate skeleton.
Then the Gloobidragon clambers onto the stage, leaving little green puddles behind it. It lets out a roar: “GLOOOBYYY!” The little ones shriek with fright and delight.
Old Mathurine, who is head of the judges, gives her spectacles a long wipe. “In a hundred years of competitions, I have never seen anything quite so disgusting. Bravo!”

Did Rosie win? She was awarded the prize for Most Original Monster! First prize went to the skeleton. But splashing in her green bath, watched by Pompon, Rosie doesn't mind a bit.
That evening, it takes three whole baths to wash away all the green. The bathwater looks just like spinach soup. Pompon pokes his tongue out, wanting a taste!
From her dragon, Rosie kept just one crinkly scale at the bottom of a box. Next year, she will make another perfect costume. But she will pack a little pot of mud. Just in case.


=== rosie_ski
titre: Rosie, Bottom of the Class

Rosie runs faster than anyone. She jumps higher, she swims further. So when she arrives at White Pines Resort, she doesn't have the slightest doubt. “Skiing? I’ll be the best by lunchtime! Ready, steady, go!”
She clicks into her skis, stands up tall. And whoosh—her two paws shoot off in different directions, as if they’re having a row. Thud!
She gets back up. Thud! She gets up again. Thud! “These skis are broken!” she grumbles. “No,” smiles Mr Cabri. “They’re brand-new. You’re the one who is new.”

The worst part is everyone else. Nestor, who is usually so slow, glides peacefully along like a big boat. Plume carves neat little turns. Even Pompon stays on his feet!
And Rosie, the fastest in the forest, spends her morning with her bottom in the snow, watching tiny toddlers whizz past. She is last. The very last in the class.
“Take your time,” Nestor tells her kindly. “Do what I do.” Taking advice from Nestor! Rosie grits her teeth. Smoke practically puffs from her ears.

At lunchtime, she throws down her ski poles. “Skiing is rubbish. It’s a silly sport. I am never doing it again!” And off she goes to sulk on the bench outside the chalet.
From there, she watches the others. They are laughing. They fall over too, actually. Nestor tumbles. Plume tumbles. But they just giggle and get back up.
“How can they laugh?” Rosie mutters, curling into a ball. Whenever she isn't the best, she feels like she isn't anything at all.

Mr Cabri comes and sits beside her with two steaming mugs of hot chocolate. “Do you know why Nestor manages so well?” “Because he’s good at it.” “No. Because he’s used to not getting things right the first time.”
“You are always first. You have never learned how to be a beginner. It’s a real job, you know. And the very best one.”
“The best one? Tumbling over all the time?” Rosie asks, surprised. “Of course. When you’re a champion, you hardly improve at all. When you’re a beginner, you make a little miracle happen every single day.”

Rosie finishes her hot chocolate. She looks at her skis, stuck in the snow. A big sigh. And back she goes. “Just so you know, I am going to fall over.” “I should hope so!” replies Mr Cabri.
She falls. Once, twice, ten times. But now, she’s counting. “Eleven!” shouts Pompon. “Twelve!” On number thirteen, she glides for three whole metres before tumbling over.
Three metres! “Did you see? Did you see?” she beams. She has snow right up to her ears, and a grin from ear to ear.

The next day, she stays upright all the way to the pine tree. The day after that, she makes her first turn. She didn't do it on purpose, but it counts all the same!
When she falls, Nestor reaches out a paw. She takes it. “How do you bend your knees like that?” It is the first time in her life that Rosie has ever asked for advice.
“As if you’re sitting on a tiny little stool,” Nestor explains. She tries it. It works! “Nestor, you’re a genius!” And Nestor blushes all the way up to his bobble hat.

At the end of the week comes the Snowflake test. You have to ski all the way down the green slope without falling once. In her chest, Rosie’s heart goes thump, thump, thump.
Off she goes. A snowplough. A turn. Another turn. She skis down slowly, much slower than the others, with her tongue poked out in concentration. She finishes last. But she finishes on her feet!
Mr Cabri pins a little silver snowflake onto her scarf. Rosie has won cups, medals, and crowns before. But none of them had ever felt like this.

On the final morning, a tiny little marmot arrives at the ski school. She clicks into her skis. Thud! She gets back up. Thud! “These skis are broken!” she whimpers.
Rosie glides over in a snowplough. She crouches down. “No. They’re brand-new. You’re the one who is new. And you’re lucky—it’s the best job in the whole world.”
“Come on, I’ll count with you. One!” Thud! “Two!” Thud! And across the beginners’ slope echo two big bursts of laughter, with both bottoms plopped right in the snow.


=== rosie_mer
titre: Rosie and the Dancing Crab

At low tide, Dunes Beach is full of treasures. Puddles, rocks, and seaweed that goes pop when you step on it. Rosie explores every little corner, bucket in paw.
And on top of a rock, she spots the funniest thing in the whole wide world. A little red crab, dancing! Three steps to the left, three steps to the right, and click, clack! go his claws.
“Ooh! I want him! Ready, steady, go!” Rosie is as quick as a flash. Whoosh! The crab is in the bucket before his dance is even finished.

“Look! I’ve got a crab!” cheers Rosie. “His name is Castanet, and he’s mine. I’m taking him home, and he will dance for me every single day.”
She lines the bucket with sand. Then she adds a seashell, just to make it pretty. And a piece of biscuit. “You’ll be ever so cosy here. Go on, dance!” And she claps her paws.
Castanet doesn't dance. He walks round the bucket. Round and round again. Then he stretches his claws towards the rim, way up high.

That evening, in the beach hut, Rosie puts the bucket on the table. “Dance, Castanet! One, two, three!” Nothing. She taps the bucket. Tap, tap. The crab scuttles under the seashell.
“Is your crab broken?” asks Pompon. “Of course not,” replies Rosie. “He’s just tired. He’ll dance tomorrow.”
But the next day, Castanet still isn't dancing. He hasn't touched the biscuit. And he isn't even very red anymore: he has turned a pale, sad little pink.

Plume leans over the bucket. She watches for a long time, as she does so well. “Rosie… have you seen where he’s looking?”
The crab’s two little eyes, on the tips of their stalks, are turned towards the window. Towards the sound of the waves. They don't move at all.
“He was dancing on his rock,” whispers Plume. “I don't think he was dancing for you. I think he was dancing because he was home.”

“But I’m taking good care of him!” protests Rosie. “He has sand, a shell, and a biscuit!” She hugs the bucket tightly, looking stubborn. “And anyway, I found him first.”
That night, a tiny sound wakes her. Scritch. Scritch. It’s Castanet, trying to climb the side of the bucket. He slips. He tries again. He slips once more.
Rosie wonders what she would do if a giant popped her into a bucket. Even a very pretty bucket. Even with crunchy carrots. She wouldn't feel like running around either.

Early the next morning, before the cottage wakes up, she takes the bucket and heads down to the beach. The tide is out. The rock is right there, all shiny and wet.
“I wanted to keep you because you were lovely,” she whispers. “But at my house, you aren't lovely at all. You’re just sad. I’m sorry, Castanet.”
She tilts the bucket, ever so gently. The little crab hesitates on the rim. Then he slides onto the rock, feeling the salty water under his feet once more. Rosie holds her breath.

At first, he doesn't move. Then one leg moves. Then another. Three steps to the left. Three steps to the right. And click, clack! His claws snap in the morning sunshine.
Out of a crack pops a second crab. Then a third. A whole merry troupe, forming a circle around him and waving their claws! Castanet has turned bright cherry-red again.
Rosie sits down on the wet sand, hugging her knees. She doesn't touch a thing. She just watches. It is ever so much lovelier than inside the bucket.

Every morning of the holidays, Rosie goes back to the rock at low tide. She sits as quiet as a mouse. And nearly every time, Castanet comes to dance. Not for her. But not far away.
She has turned her bucket upside down. It makes a wonderful stool for peering into rock pools. Inside it, she only ever brings home empty shells, where nobody lives.
And when a toddler arrives with a fishing net, shouting, “Ooh! A crab! I want him!”, Rosie makes room for them on the sand. “Shh. Sit down. Look. He’s going to dance.”
