=== plume_pirates
titre: Plume and the Tiny Wee Voice

On board The Seashell, when night falls, the whole crew goes to sleep. The whole crew except Plume, perched high up the mast with her lantern. An owl at night sees absolutely everything.
From up there, she sees the fish sparkling under the hull. She sees shooting stars dash across the sky. And she even sees the captain tiptoeing on her paws to gobble up the rest of the cake.
Plume sees it all. But she doesn't breathe a single word. Her voice is so tiny, so squeaky-small, that she worries nobody will hear her. Or worse: that they will hear her and laugh.

By day, on deck, it's a contest to see who can shout the loudest. Captain Mirabelle thunders. The sailors bellow their sea shanties. Sardine squeaks. What a racket! You couldn't hear yourself sneeze.
Plume raises a wing. “Hoo... excuse me... I think the rope has come undone...” Nobody hears her. Flap! The sail drops right on top of the cook. All that's poking out is a snout and a ladle.
“You should have said something!” grumble the sailors. “I did say something...” murmurs Plume with her head down. But nobody hears that either.

One night, the fog rolls in. Thick, grey and woolly. It swallows the sea, it swallows the stars. The sailors are snoring away. Plume hops up into the crow's nest and opens her big round eyes wide.
And there, in the middle of all that grey, she spots a shadow. A big black shadow, without a single lantern, gliding across the water without a sound. A boat!
Standing at the bow is a cat with a sneaky grin and a patch over one eye. Clawford! That cheeky scoundrel is sneaking over to pinch their treasure while everybody sleeps.

Plume's heart beats like a little drum. Quick as a flash, she flutters down the mast to the captain's hammock. “Hoo... Captain... there's a...”, she whispers right in her ear.
“Snxxx... pheeew...” The captain rolls over without opening an eye. Plume tries with Nestor. “Hoo... please wake up...” The big bear smiles happily in his sleep. He's dreaming of honey.
On deck, Plume freezes. The black boat is very close now. So close that she can hear Clawford's claws scraping the wood. Skrrrrtch!

Plume trembles from her beak to her tail feathers. What can she do? Ring the big alarm bell? Far too heavy for her wings. Bang the drum? She hasn't got any drumsticks.
All she has left is her voice. Her tiny wee voice. The one nobody ever hears. Plume rests a wing against her throat. “I'll never manage,” she thinks.
A grey paw reaches over the ship's rail. Then another. And a yellow eye glints through the fog, as round as a shiny gold coin.

So Plume flies right to the very top of the mast. She puffs out her feathers, she puffs out her chest. Then she thinks of all the words she never dared say, all the words stuck in her throat, and she gathers them all in her beak.
“HOOOOO-HOOOOO! THIEVES! HOOOOO-HOOOOO!” Her cry rolls through the fog just like a foghorn. Plume's eyes go wide: she had no idea she had all that inside her!
Crash, bang, wallop! The captain tumbles out of her hammock, paws in the air. Nestor leaps up. Sardine waves a fork. “ALL HANDS ON DECK! PIRATE ALERT!”

Clawford has never had such a fright in all his nine lives. A ghost is shrieking in the sails, he's sure of it! His claws slip and... splash! Away he swims back to his boat, soaked right down to his whiskers.
“Who raised the alarm?” wonders the captain, lifting her lantern. “What a voice! What a set of lungs!” Everyone looks around, shrugging their shoulders. And high up on the mast, a tiny owl shyly lifts a wing.
“It was me...” It's only a whisper. But this time, a great hush falls over the deck, and every single sailor hears.

Ever since that night, Plume has been the official lookout on The Seashell. No need to shout: the moment she raises a wing, the crew stop singing straight away and prick up their ears.
“The rope is loose.” “There's a sandbank on the left.” “Captain, you've finished the cake again.” Mirabelle blushes, with crumbs all over her whiskers.
Her voice is still quiet and gentle. But nowadays, everyone leans in to listen. And on some moonlit nights, right at the top of the mast, she closes her eyes and lets out a big, happy “Hoo-hoo!” to no one at all. Just for fun.


=== plume_dino
titre: Plume and the Thousand Whys

In Fern Valley, Plume has a collection. Not pebbles, nor seashells. A collection of questions, which she gathers everywhere she goes.
“Why do ferns uncurl? Why does Tric have three horns and not four? Why does the volcano snore? And why do we say why?” Tric is left completely flabbergasted.
She asks a hundred every single day. At breakfast, at naptime, and even when Tric, curled up under his fern, would really rather sleep.

“Enough with your whys!” groans Tric, covering his ears. “You're making my horns ache.” “Things are just the way they are,” grunts Grondar the tyrannosaurus. “End of story.”
“We’ve always done it this way,” the grown-ups reply with a shrug. That is the answer Plume likes least of all.
So one day, Plume decides to stop. She folds up her questions, packs them right at the back of her head, wraps herself in her wings, and shuts her beak. Snap!

The valley is very peaceful without all the whys. Tric has a snooze. Grondar does too. But Plume's head is tickling, just like your nose when you are holding back a sneeze.
The thing is, she notices some rather funny things. This morning, all the birds zoomed off towards the sea. Why? The frogs are leaving the pond in single file. Why?
And the river! Plume dips the tip of a wing in: the water is as warm as a bath. Why, why, why? The questions knock inside her head. Tap, tap, tap.

Plume cannot hold it in any longer. With a flap of her wings, she flies to Granny Dipla, the oldest and wisest dinosaur in the valley. “Granny... I know I shouldn’t... but I’ve got three whys tickling me,” she confesses.
Granny Dipla lowers her long, long neck down to the rock. “Heh heh. Hand them over, sweetheart. It’s been ages since anyone asked me a proper question,” she chuckles.
Plume explains everything, flapping her wings wide. The birds. The frogs. The warm water. Granny Dipla listens without interrupting. And the more she listens, the further her glasses slide down her nose.

“Warm water... The birds...” mutters the old lady. “My grandmother used to tell me about that, when I was only knee-high to a fern.” Slowly, she lifts her head towards the mountain.
The Big Snorer, the old volcano, is not snoring anymore. It is smoking. A plume of black smoke, rising straight into the sky. “It is going to wake up, my dear,” whispers Granny Dipla. “Tonight, perhaps.”
She nuzzles Plume with the tip of her snout. “Everyone saw the birds leave. But no one asked why. No one, except you.”

They must warn the whole valley! Granny Dipla is far too slow. So Plume swoops off, from fern to fern, right onto Tric’s head. “Wake up! The volcano! We have to leave!”
“You again?” yawns Tric, opening his jaws wide. “And why would we leave?” Plume stamps her feet between his horns. “Aha! You see, that’s a good question! Touch the river.”
Tric dips a paw in. Ouch, it’s hot! He looks at the smoke, he looks at Plume, and his eyes go round as saucers. “GRONDAR! Get up! Plume was right!”

The whole valley sets off towards Blue Hill. Grondar carries the eggs close to his chest, ever so gently, without cracking a single one. Granny Dipla has hoisted the little ones onto her back. And right out in front, Plume leads the way.
They reach the top just as the sun is setting. And then... BOOM! The Big Snorer spits a shower of sparks. The lava flows, red and slow, right where they were sleeping only this morning.
Nobody says a word. It is beautiful and terrifying all at once. Grondar sets the eggs down in the grass. Everyone is there, safe and sound. Not a single scale is missing.

In the dark, a deep voice clears its throat. It is Grondar, feeling rather shy. “Tell me, Plume... Why does lava make light?”
“And why does smoke go up?” adds Tric. “And why don't the stars fall down?” squeaks a tiny dinosaur, nose pointed at the sky. Plume's eyes go wide with delight.
“I haven't the foggiest!” she cries, opening her wings wide. “But we are going to find out!” And that night, up on the hill, nobody sleeps a wink. There are far too many whys to share.


=== plume_chevaliers
titre: Plume the Apprentice Magician

Right at the top of Highrock Castle, in the leaning tower, lives Master Balthazar. He is an old raven wizard who wears a star-spangled hat, with pockets always full of crumbs.
Since Monday, he has had an apprentice: Plume. She has read all the big spellbooks and knows every magic rhyme right to the tips of her feathers. All she needs to do now is have a go.
“First spell,” announces Master Balthazar, “making a feather fly. It’s the easiest one.” Plume squeezes her eyes tightly shut and raises her wand. “Abracadabri, abracadabra!”

Poof! The feather does not fly. It turns into a frog. A big, bumpy green frog. “Croak,” grumbles the frog, not happy at all.
Plume tries again. Poof! This time, a cloud bursts right above her head: it’s raining soup! She tries again. Poof! Master Balthazar’s hat begins to sing. Terribly out of tune.
“Never mind,” says the old raven gently, dabbing the soup off his feathers. “Try again.” But Plume’s eyes are prickling with tears. In the books, it always worked on the very first try.

On Tuesday, she gets it wrong nine times. The feather turns into a sausage, a cloud, and an old sock. On Wednesday, she gets it wrong seventeen times. And on Thursday, oh dear, she gets herself wrong: there she is, bright blue from head to claws!
Down in the courtyard, everyone starts to giggle. “Look, it’s raining soup! That little owl is at it again!” Sir Gontran, the peacock, even makes up a silly song and squawks it for all to hear.
That evening, Plume drops her wand on the table. “I’m no good at this,” she sniffs. “I’ll never manage it. Real magicians never make mistakes.”

That night, she packs her little bundle. Three books, a hazelnut, and that is all. Then she creeps down the spiral staircase, step by step, on the very tips of her toes.
But waiting on the bottom step is someone unexpected. It is Monday’s frog. “Croak. Before you run away, you ought to take a peek inside the big chest upstairs. The one Balthazar never shows to anyone.”
Plume hesitates. A secret chest? But why? She simply cannot resist: back up she creeps.

The lid creaks open. Inside, there is no gold or jewels. Just notebooks. Dozens of old, dog-eared notebooks, covered in messy scribbles and messy splatters.
By the glow of her lantern, Plume reads: “Attempt number 1: failed. The feather caught fire. Attempt number 2: failed. Turned my teacher into a teapot. Attempt number 212: failed, but a bit better.”
She turns the pages. Failed. Failed. Failed. Then she closes the notebook. On the cover, written by a very little crow, is a name: Balthazar.

“Ah. You found my treasures.” Master Balthazar is standing there in his nightshirt, resting a gentle wing on her shoulder. “Four hundred and three tries before I could make my first feather float. I kept every single mistake, like a treasure.”
“But… why keep all the mistakes?” The old raven hugs a notebook close to his heart. “Because they are what taught me everything. The burning feather taught me not to shout. The teapot taught me to speak clearly.”
He gives her a warm wink. “A magician who never makes a mistake doesn't exist, Plume. Or if they do, it means they have stopped trying new things.”

The next morning, Plume opens a brand-new notebook. In her neatest handwriting, she writes: “Attempt number 31.” A flick of her wand… Poof! A carrot. So she writes down: “Failed. Said abracadabri too fast.”
Attempt 32: the feather quivers. Attempt 40: it lifts by an inch, wobbles, and flops back down. Plume does not cry anymore. She takes notes, she thinks, and she tries again.
And then, one morning… “Abracadabri, abracadabra.” The feather lifts. Up, up it floats, twirling and dancing in a beam of golden sunlight, right before Master Balthazar’s eyes. She did it! It is flying!

Plume opens the window wide. The feather flutters out, followed by a hundred more, then a thousand more, swirling high above the castle like a flurry of summer snow.
Down below, nobody is teasing anymore. Everyone is watching, mouths wide open. Even Sir Gontran. Especially Sir Gontran, who is craning his neck to see where all the gorgeous feathers from his tail have gone!
That night, by candlelight, Plume writes in her notebook: “Attempt number 58: hooray!” She draws a neat little star. Then she adds, in tiny letters: “Tomorrow, I will try something I don't know how to do yet.”


=== plume_espace
titre: Plume and Zig, the Alien Who Spoke in Colours

At Three Moons Station, a newcomer has just arrived. His name is Zig. His home planet is so far away, so lost in the depths of space, that it doesn't appear on any map at all.
Zig is green, squishy and wobbly like jelly. His three eyes, perched on three long stalks, look off in every direction. And best of all, Zig doesn't talk. Not a single word.
Instead, he lights up! Yellow, blue, pink, drifting across his body like little clouds. Plume has never seen anything quite so pretty.

“Hello there!” calls Filou. “Do you want to play?” Zig turns bright yellow. “He’s not answering. He's weird,” grumbles the little fox. “Would you like a biscuit?” offers Nestor. Zig turns orange, with polka dots.
“He didn't even say thank you!” After a week, nobody talks to him any more. “Leave him be, he doesn't understand anything.” And they play ball without him.
Zig stays curled up in his corner, by the big porthole. He is a dull, gloomy blue-grey, the colour of a rainy day. Nobody wonders what it means. Nobody, except Plume.

Plume isn't very chatty either. But she watches closely. And by watching, she has noticed something: Zig's colours never appear by chance.
When the ball rolls near him: yellow. When the air smells of delicious cake: spotted orange. And when the door slams shut… bang! Violet, all in a flash.
Plume opens her little notebook. Very neatly, she writes it down: “Yellow, happy? Orange, yummy? Violet, scared?” And she comes back the next day. And the day after that. And the day after that, too.

One morning, she sits down right beside him, without making a sound. Zig turns a little bit violet. She doesn't move; she just waits. Very gently, the violet fades away.
Then Plume takes out her torch, slips a piece of yellow paper over the front, and switches it on. Click! A round beam of yellow light. Zig’s three eyes swivel towards her, all at the same time.
He looks at the torch, and then he looks at Plume. Suddenly, his whole body blazes with brilliant yellow, crackling with little sparks. Now that isn't in the notebook. But Plume understands, loud and clear.

From then on, they chat every single day. Plume with her coloured papers, and Zig with his skin. Light blue: “How are you?” Pink: “I like you.” Blinking green: “Tickles!”
Zig teaches her colours she has never seen before. There is one, between blue and gold, that shimmers whenever he gazes at the stars. It means: “I miss my home.” Plume thinks it is ever so beautiful, and ever so sad.
She answers him in pink. Zig snuggles close to her, right under her wing. He is warm, just like a hot-water bottle.

One evening, snap! The whole station goes pitch black. No lights. No radio. A giant power cut! The little astronauts grope about, bumping into walls in the dark. “I'm scared!” “Where is the door?”
They call out and shout, but their voices get lost down the long corridors. Nobody knows which way to go.
And yet, deep in the dark, a soft green glow is pulsing. There is someone here who doesn't need a torch to make himself understood.

Zig lights up. Bright! Brighter than a nightlight, brighter than a lighthouse beam. “Follow him!” calls Plume. “But we don't understand a word he's saying!” Filou cries. “I do.”
Zig turns green. “Green means: forward march!” translates Plume. Red. “Red means: stop, mind the step.” Swirling blue. “Turn left!” And the whole line follows the little light, in single file, holding paws.
They burst into the great hall just as the power hums back on. Everyone is there, safe and sound! Zig twinkles in every colour all at once, a magical swirl that even Plume has never seen. She guesses what it means: he is very, very proud.

The next day, Filou arrives with a yellow paper stuck to his forehead. “Does this really mean hello?” he asks nervously. As for Nestor, he has baked an orange cake with spots.
Now, at Three Moons Station, everyone speaks a little bit of Zig. And Zig has learned a word of his own. Just one. He whispers it ever so softly, in a tiny bubble of a voice: “Plume.”
And whenever he says it, he turns a lovely shade of pink.


=== plume_foret
titre: Plume, Hazel and the Treehouse Split in Two

In Four Oaks Forest, Plume the owl and Hazel the squirrel have always been friends. Ever since long before one learned to fly, and the other to climb.
This summer, they have a grand project: to build the finest treehouse in the forest, right up high in the old oak tree. Hazel scrambles up, her arms full of branches. Plume draws the plans.
From morning till night, they are busy as bees. They even have a secret password: “Carabistouille!” Just saying it makes them giggle and chuckle until their tummies ache.

One day, the treehouse is almost finished. All it needs now is the flag. “We’re putting up a red leaf,” Plume decides. “The treehouse was my idea.”
“Your idea?” Hazel gasps. “I carried all the branches! We’re putting up a jay’s feather, blue as the sky.” “My idea!” “My branches!”
Each pulls from her own side. Crack! The stick snaps clean in two. “You’re not my friend anymore!” “Neither are you!” The words tumble out so fast they hang in the air between them, completely astonished.

Hazel draws a white line on the floor, right down the middle of the treehouse. “That is my side.” “Fine,” Plume replies, crossing her wings. “And that is mine. You’re not allowed to look out of my window!”
There they stand, beak to nose, each on her own side, without a word. Plume has the plans, but no more branches. Hazel has the branches, but no more plans.
On the first day, they sulk splendidly, chins held high, cheeks puffed out. It’s actually rather nice, sulking. At first.

On the second day, Plume spots a snail wearing a mushroom, just like a little hat. Quickly, she turns round to tell Hazel… Oh no. That’s right.
On the other side of the line, Hazel finds a hazelnut shaped like a heart. She half turns, opens her mouth… and closes it again.
It’s funny how much you have to tell someone, when you’re not speaking to them anymore.

“She should say sorry first,” Plume grumbles at her window. “She was the one who shouted first.” “She should say sorry first,” Hazel grumbles at hers. “She started it.”
Old Mathurine the tortoise ambles by, taking tiny little steps. She looks up. “Well, well! Two half-treehouses. And two half-smiles. What a shame, they used to make a whole.”
Plume pretends not to hear. But she thinks about it all night long, her eyes wide open.

On the third day, it begins to rain. Heavy summer rain, drumming on the leaves. The roof is only half-finished on each side. Drip, drop, it trickles onto Plume. Drip, drop, it trickles onto Hazel.
Plume shivers. At her feet, the white line is melting into little puddles: the rain is washing it away. Then she looks at Hazel’s back, all wet, all small.
Saying sorry first is like jumping into cold water. You’re scared beforehand. Plume takes a deep breath and reaches out a wing. “Hazel… Carabistouille?”

Hazel whips round with a leap. Her whiskers quiver. “Carabistouille,” she whispers back. And just like that, they burst out laughing, with a few tears too, but in the rain nobody can tell.
“I’m sorry. They were your branches.” “I’m sorry. It was your idea.” Forehead to forehead, they whisper together: “It was our treehouse.”
Hazel holds the branches. Plume points out where to put them. In ten minutes, the roof is finished. Not a single drop gets through: they are snug, dry, and safe.

When the sun returns, bringing a rainbow with it, they hoist the flag. A red leaf and a jay’s feather, tied together. It looks much prettier.
Nothing is left of the line on the floor. Just a tiny mark in that very spot, which no one else would ever notice. But they do.
They still bicker, of course. Over a bilberry, over a song, over who spotted the snail first. But never for three days. Three days is far too long without saying “Carabistouille”.


=== plume_ocean
titre: Plume and the Grey Reef

Every summer, Plume slips on her little glass helmet and dives down to see her friends at Thousand Bubbles Reef. Of all the places in the world, it is the most colourful.
There are pink corals, waving orange anemones, and fish striped just like sweets. And tiny Pearl the seahorse, who guides her all around.
But this summer, when Plume arrives, something is wrong. She looks around, squinting her eyes. Why is that corner so pale? Suddenly she realises: the colours are missing.

A whole corner of the reef has turned grey, dull as ash. Trapped among the corals are things that do not grow in the sea. A bottle. A tin can. An old shoe.
“It tumbles down from up above,” sighs Pearl. “More and more every day. The clownfish packed their bags and moved away. And the anemone won't open anymore.”
A bag floats in the water, bobbing about like a jellyfish. But it isn't a jellyfish. A little turtle swims up, feeling hungry, and opens her beak to take a bite…

“No!” Plume swoops in and snatches the bag just in time. The little turtle blinks, very surprised. “Isn't that food?” “Oh no. Not at all.”
Plume looks at the bag at the tip of her wing. Then at the reef. There is rubbish everywhere, as far as the eye can see. Hundreds of bits.
“We have to clear it all away!” she decides, spreading her wings wide. The fish look at each other, then swim backwards. “All of it? But that's impossible. We're too small. And there's far too much.”

Even Octave, the octopus with spectacles, shakes his head. “I tried, with all eight of my arms. The next day, just as much fell down again. What's the point?” he sighs.
Plume doesn't know what to say. It is true that she is small. It is true that there is far too much. Thoughtfully, she looks down at the tin can right by her feet.
So she picks it up and slips it into her net. “There,” she announces quietly. “That's one less.”

She picks up another. And another. All morning, without giving up, she fills her net while the fish watch. “She'll never manage it,” they whisper behind their fins.
By midday, Plume has cleared a patch no bigger than a dinner plate. A dinner plate is very small. But right in the middle, a coral has got a little pink back in its cheeks.
Pearl swims over. Tiny as she is, she can only carry a bottle top. But she carries it anyway, bravely. “Two less,” she declares.

The little turtle comes to lend a flipper. Then a crab, carrying tin cans on his shell. Then two, then ten. “Well,” grumbles Octave, grabbing a bottle. “Eight arms doing nothing is a terrible waste.”
The fish make a chain, fin to fin. The prawns do the sorting. The crabs do the carrying. Even the grumpy old moray eel, who likes nobody, pokes her nose out of her hole to nudge a shoe along.
No one sighs, “We're too small,” anymore. There is no time for that! They count with their claws in the air: “A hundred less! Two hundred less!”

In the evening, Plume swims back up to the surface, dragging her bursting net behind her. On the beach, a big walrus in a striped swimsuit is finishing his picnic. There he is, lifting his empty bottle, ready to toss it into the waves.
Plume doesn't shout. She places the net right in front of him, and she tells him everything. The turtle. The anemone. The grey coral. The walrus looks down at his bottle. His big cheeks turn bright red.
“I didn't know it went anywhere,” he mumbles, looking very sheepish. And he takes it home with him. The next day, on the beach, someone has put up a big bin. With a fish painted on it.

The following summer, when Plume puts on her helmet and dives, her heart beats a little fast. What if everything has turned grey again? Down at the bottom, Pearl and Octave are already waiting for her.
But Thousand Bubbles Reef has got its pinks back, its oranges, and its sweet-shop stripes. The clownfish have moved back in. And the anemone blooms wide, as if to say hello.
Now and then, a bottle still tumbles down. But whoever spots it first zooms off to pick it up. “One less!” they sing out. It has become the motto of the reef.


=== plume_noel
titre: Plume and the Dark Window

On Christmas Eve, the whole of Four Oaks Forest is sparkling. Lanterns sway gently in the trees, candles glow in the windows, and holly garlands dress the doors.
Plume glides over the rooftops, counting the lights. One hundred and twelve, one hundred and thirteen… And there, by the frozen pond, stands a house that is completely dark. Not a single candle. Not a single garland.
It is the home of Mr Grumbles, the old badger. “Don’t go there,” warns Filou, shaking his head. “He hates Christmas. He hates everyone. He even yelled at me, just for a tiny snowball!”

But Plume has a question tickling her thoughts. “Why? Why would anyone hate Christmas?” She lands on the doormat and knocks. Knock, knock.
The door creeps open just a crack. An eye gleams behind a pair of spectacles. “No carols! No biscuits! No merry this-and-that! Shoo!” And slam! Plume jumps, her feathers all ruffled.
Thoughtfully, she stays on the doormat. Behind him, on the mantelpiece, she had just enough time to spot something. A picture frame. With two badgers inside.

Plume knows very well that you shouldn't peek through windows. She peeks anyway. Just a little peek.
Mr Grumbles is sitting in his armchair, quite still, his paws resting on his knees. Opposite him sits a second armchair, empty, with some knitting folded on it. He isn't reading. He isn't doing anything at all. He is just gazing at the frame.
Then Plume understands. He doesn't hate Christmas. Christmas simply reminds him of someone who isn't here anymore. And shouting is easier than crying.

She tells her friends all about it. “We could sing him a carol outside his window!” cries Rosie. “Really loudly!” “And bring him an enormous cake!” adds Nestor.
Plume gently shakes her head. “He said no carols, no biscuits. We have to listen to him.” She closes her eyes and thinks for a long time. Plume's best ideas are often very little ones.
She chooses a lantern. Just one. She lights it, then carries it over to place it on the dark windowsill. Without knocking. Without a word. And she flutters away into the snow.

Hidden behind a fir tree, she watches. The door opens. Mr Grumbles discovers the lantern. He grunts, peering left and right. Nobody.
He puffs out his cheeks to blow it out… and stops. The tiny flame dances in his spectacles. So he stands there for a long time in the cold, watching it flicker.
Then, ever so gently, he picks it up and carries it inside. Through the window, Plume sees him place it on the mantelpiece. Right beside the frame.

On Christmas night, Plume comes back. This time, the door opens before she can even knock. “You're the lantern.” “Yes, sir.” “Hmph. You'll catch a chill,” he grumbles. “Come in. But no carols.”
Inside, it smells of dust and herbal tea. Mr Grumbles pours her a steaming cup. They don't talk. Plume is very good at saying nothing at all. In fact, it is her speciality.
After a while, he is the one who speaks. “Her name was Hortense. She used to put candles everywhere. Even in the bathtub.” And he chuckles, a rusty little laugh that hadn't been used in a very long time.

He gets up and lifts the lid of an old chest. Asleep inside are dozens of candles and a dried-up holly garland. “She would have said my house is scaring the stars away.”
Together, they light the candles. One in every window. One on every stair step. And one in the bathtub, of course, because that was the tradition.
On the other side of the pond, the woodland folk look up. “Look! Over at Mr Grumbles' house!” The ice is glowing with light. One hundred and fourteen lights. Every single one is counted.

Filou, Rosie and Nestor arrive on tiptoe, carrying a humble little cake. Mr Grumbles frowns. “I did warn you: no biscuits.” “It's a cake, sir.” “Hmph. Well, that's alright then.”
They snuggle close by the fire, huddled together on the rug. The second armchair stays empty, with its knitting. Nobody sits there. But it looks less lonely now, with all these little friends around.
“Right then,” grunts Mr Grumbles. “One carol. Just one. And not too loud.” They sing ever so softly, like a lullaby. And when they finish, he is the one who asks, “Again.”


=== plume_halloween
titre: Plume and the Biggest Pumpkin

In the spring, Plume and Hazel each plant a pumpkin seed, side by side in the fresh, cool soil. It is for the big Halloween contest.
Plume does everything by the book. She waters in the morning, talks to her shoot in the evening, and measures her pumpkin every day with a bit of string.
Then autumn arrives. Plume’s pumpkin is as small as an apple. Hazel’s is as big as a wheelbarrow! “Why is that?” wonders Plume.

Everyone comes running to admire Hazel’s pumpkin. “What a beauty!” “She’s going to win, for sure!” Hazel hops with joy.
Plume whispers, “Well done.” But at the very same moment, something tightens in her tummy. A little pinch. Cold, sharp, and a little bit green.
The pinch whispers in her ear. “It’s not fair. She didn’t even read the books. It’s just pure luck, that’s all. You deserve to win much more than her.”

Over the next few days, the pinch grows bigger. When Hazel comes over to play, Plume turns her back: she is busy. When Hazel laughs, Plume thinks she is laughing too loudly.
“Are you cross?” asks Hazel gently. “No.” “Are you sure?” “I said no!” It isn’t a real no. But how can she tell her best friend: I feel sad that you are happy?
The night before the contest, Plume cannot sleep. With a flap of her wings, she flits down to the vegetable patch. The big pumpkin glows softly under the moon.

It sits right at the top of a little slope, held in place by a single stone. The pinch whispers: “If that stone moved… it would roll right into the stream. Splash! No one would ever know.”
Plume rests a claw on the stone. It is cold. Just one tiny push would be enough.
Then she thinks of Hazel, who hauled heavy watering cans all summer with her little paws. She thinks of Hazel’s face tomorrow morning, looking at an empty patch. Plume pulls her claw back with a jump, as if the stone were burning hot.

She perches on the fence, trembling all over. “What was I about to do?” Inside, she feels awful. If only she could pluck out this pinch and throw it far, far away!
“Plume?” comes a little voice in the dark, and the flicker of a candle. It is Hazel in her nightie, who could not sleep either. “What are you doing out here?”
Plume could make up an excuse. Instead, she looks right at her friend, takes a deep breath, and tells the truth—the kind that is so hard to let out.

“I’m jealous. Your pumpkin is enormous, and mine is ridiculous. I have a green pinch in my tummy, and it gives me horrible ideas. I’m sorry, Hazel.”
Hazel climbs up to sit beside her. For a moment, she stays quiet, looking up at the stars. Then she says, “Well, I get a pinch every time you fly away.”
“What?” gasps Plume. “You can fly. You can see the whole forest from above. I have to look up from down here, and I think: it’s not fair. I was always too shy to tell you.”

They look at each other. Two friends, two pinches. Suddenly it all seems rather silly, and they start to giggle softly, so as not to wake the slugs.
The next day, Hazel’s pumpkin wins first prize. Plume claps with both wings. The pinch is still there, all shrivelled up. But now that she has spoken it out loud, it is no longer the boss.
“What about yours?” Hazel cries. “We can’t leave it all alone!” Crouching down, they look at the apple-pumpkin. It really is tiny.

Together, they scoop it out. They carve two eyes and a smile, and then pop a candle inside. It is the teeniest Halloween lantern in the whole forest.
And because it weighs almost nothing at all, Plume can carry it up into the air. “Hop on!” she calls. Hazel climbs onto her back, holds on with all her might, and peeks out.
That evening, everyone below sees a little orange star zooming over the treetops. And way up high, two voices laughing. As for the giant pumpkin, it stayed behind in the vegetable patch. It was far too heavy for an adventure like that.


=== plume_ski
titre: Plume and Her First Night Away from the Nest

For the very first time in her life, Plume is going on holiday without her parents. A whole week at White Pines Resort, with her friends, in a big wooden chalet.
In her suitcase, she has packed her hat, her mittens, and three books. And Raggy, her cuddly toy, she tucked right at the bottom so no one would see. She is a big girl now.
“You’ll see, it’s going to be wonderful,” Mum promises, hugging her tight. Plume nods. Then, from the back of the sledge, she waves her wing until Mum is just a tiny little speck.

The day is wonderful. There is snow right up to the windows! They build a snowman, with a carrot for a beak. Then they whizz down the slope on their sledges, whooping with joy.
In the evening, they tuck into hot soup and melted cheese, and Mr Cabri tells the story of the yeti who was ticklish. Plume laughs so much that she gets the hiccups.
She doesn’t think about home even once. Not once. Until the lights are switched off.

In the dormitory, her friends fall asleep straight away. Nestor snores. Rosie wiggles. Filou mumbles in his sleep. But Plume lies with her eyes wide open.
This bed doesn’t smell like her bed. The ceiling beams creak. There is no sound of Daddy tidying the kitchen, and no little ray of light peeking under the door.
A lump rises in her throat. It is big, it is warm, and it has a name Plume didn’t know yet: nest-sickness.

She wants to go home. Right now. To fly all the way home, even through the night, even through the snow. But it is too far, and her wings are too small.
Very gently, she opens her suitcase a crack. She rummages under the mittens, pulls out Raggy, and hugs him tight. He smells of home. And then, the tears just start to flow.
She cries quietly, curled up beneath her blanket, so she won’t wake anyone. “I’m such a baby,” she thinks. “The others are fast asleep. I’m the only one who’s sad.”

“Plume?” comes a whisper from the bunk below. It’s Nestor. He isn’t snoring any more. “Are you crying?” Quickly, Plume hides Raggy under her blanket. “No. I just have... a cold.”
Silence. Then something climbs up over the edge of the bed. A worn-out bit of blue cloth, with a chewed-up ear. “Um... This is Bidou. I never sleep without him. Don’t tell Filou.”
Plume sniffs. She pulls Raggy back out. “This is Raggy.” In the dark, the two cuddly toys say hello, nose to nose. Things feel a tiny bit better already.

The door creaks open. It’s Mr Cabri doing his rounds, carrying a lantern by his hoof. He spots their watery eyes straight away. He doesn’t say, “Come on, you’re big birds now.” He simply sits on the edge of the bed.
“The first night is always the hardest. You miss home.” “Why?” sniffs Plume. “Because you love it. That’s rather good news, isn’t it?” She hadn’t thought of it like that.
He pulls back the curtain. The moon is there, big and round, above the snowy pines. “Look at it closely. Your mummy can see it too, right now. It’s the very same moon. You’re looking at the exact same spot.”

Plume gazes at the moon with all her might. She pictures Mummy at the nest window, with her knitting. In her throat, the lump melts a little, like an ice cube in warm soup.
“Goodnight, Mummy,” she whispers. She drifts off to sleep, Raggy tucked under her wing. Down below, Nestor is already snoring, his nose buried in Bidou.
The next morning, she writes a postcard. “Dear Mummy, dear Daddy. I missed you very much last night. Today, I’m learning the snowplough. Lots of love, Plume.” And off she dashes outside.

The second night is gentler. The third night too. Every evening, when the moon comes out, the lump returns a little. But now, Plume knows what to do.
At the end of the week, when the sledge brings her back, Mummy is waiting on the platform. Plume throws herself into her wings. “I missed you!” “I missed you too, my little owl. Every night, I looked at the moon.”
Plume’s eyes go wide. Then she unpacks her suitcase. This time, Raggy isn’t hidden at the bottom. He sits proudly right at the top, in plain sight. He went skiing too. And he has lots and lots of stories to tell.


=== plume_mer
titre: Plume and the Red Kite

It is the first day of the holidays at Dunes Beach. Plume does not know anyone. She has settled down in the shade of the parasol, with a book she isn't reading.
Because a little further away, three children are playing. A little otter, a seagull, and a crab. They are galloping after a bright red kite, laughing their heads off.
Plume peeks at them over her book. She would love to play with them. But how do you join in when a game has already started?

In her head, she practises what to say. “Hello, my name is Plume. Can I play with you?” She repeats it ten times. Twenty times. She knows it by heart.
She stands up and takes three steps. Her heart is thumping right up to her ears. “What if they say no? What if they laugh? What if they don't even hear me?”
She turns around and sits back down, feeling ever so glum. Her book is upside down. She does not even notice.

On the second day, it is just the same. She edges a little closer. Right beside them, she gathers seashells, hoping they will notice her. They do not notice.
“Perhaps they’ll come and ask me,” she hopes. She waits. She builds a little mound of sand. She waits some more.
But they are totally wrapped up in their game. It isn't that they don't want her. They don't even know she is there.

On the third day, the wind picks up. The red kite tugs and tugs at its string. And suddenly—snap! The string breaks. The kite swoops away over the dunes.
The three children dash after it. Up it goes, twirling round and round, until it gets stuck right at the top of the big pine tree, on the very highest branch.
“Oh no!” The otter jumps with her arms stretched high. The crab pinches the trunk. The little seagull flaps his wings, but he cannot fly yet. The kite is much too high.

Under her parasol, Plume has not missed a thing. And she knows one thing: she can fly. In ten seconds flat, she could fetch it down.
But to do that, she would have to go over there. Right in front of them. Without her little speech, which has flown out of her head along with the kite. Her feet feel as heavy as two buckets of wet sand.
The little otter has sat down at the foot of the pine tree. Her chin is wobbling. Plume looks at that wobbly chin. And her feet lift up all on their own.

Without a word, she runs, spreads her wings, and rises into the air. Higher than the parasol. Higher than the dune. Right up to the top branch of the big pine tree.
The kite is completely tangled. With the tip of her beak, Plume untangles the string, patiently, knot after knot. Down below, three little faces watch her, open-mouthed.
She glides back down, the red kite between her feet, and lands right in front of them. She must say something. Anything at all. “Here you are,” she stammers. “It… it was up there.”

“YOU CAN FLY!” chirps the little seagull at the top of his voice. “Did you see how she flies?” “What's your name?” asks the otter. “Plume.” “I'm Lila. This is Pinchy, and this is Gull-Who-Can't-Fly-Yet.”
“Want to play with us?” asks Lila. Just like that. Simply. As if it were the easiest thing in the world.
Plume thinks of her lovely sentence, practised twenty times. She didn't need it after all. Just a few words were enough, and she wasn't even the one who said them. “Yes,” she says.

The rest of the holiday zooms by. They fly the kite higher than ever: Plume holds it, way up in the sky. She teaches the little seagull how to flap his wings. He lifts off by three whole feathers!
On the last evening, they swap seashells so they won't forget each other. “Are you coming back next summer?” “Promise.” Lila gives Plume hers, the prettiest of all, with a little hole to thread a string through.
On the train home, Plume hugs it close to her heart. At the station, an unfamiliar little owl is sitting on a bench, all by herself, watching the others play. Plume takes a deep breath. And off she goes.
